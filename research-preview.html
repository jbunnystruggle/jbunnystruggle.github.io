The Parent-Shaped Hole in K-12 AI Education

A mixed-methods research study on why parents are the missing stakeholder in school AI policy — and what districts and edtech companies need to do about it.

80 survey respondents
8 in-depth interviews
3 secondary data sources triangulated
13% of parents received a clear AI policy from their school
48% don't know if their kids use AI for schoolwork
12% received any guidance on negative impacts of AI

The Problem

Schools are integrating AI tools faster than they're building communication infrastructure to support families. The result: students are using AI in ways most parents don't know about, can't assess, and can't contextualize at home.

This isn't a literacy gap. It's a communication vacuum — one that districts are creating through inaction and that edtech companies are reinforcing by treating parents as an afterthought in product design. Closing it requires both policy and product changes.

"The parent-shaped hole in K-12 AI education isn't a knowledge problem — it's a design problem. Districts are shipping AI policy to students and staff while skipping the stakeholder who has to reinforce it at home."

Key Findings

13%
Only 13% of parents received a clear AI policy from their school

Despite rapid K-12 AI adoption, most schools haven't built the communication infrastructure to tell parents what tools are in use, under what conditions, and what protections are in place for students.

48%
Nearly half of parents don't know if their child uses AI for schoolwork

This isn't parental indifference. When the school hasn't named AI as a tool in use, parents have no reason to ask — and students have no framework for disclosure.

12%
Only 12% received guidance on negative impacts

Even among parents who did receive some AI communication, it focused almost exclusively on capability — what AI can do — not on child-specific risks: misinformation, over-reliance, privacy exposure, or developmental impact.

Primary research triangulated with Center for Democracy & Technology, Common Sense Media, and Walton-GSV-Gallup national data.

Methodology

📋

Quantitative Survey

80 respondents; parents of K-12 students across multiple school settings. Questions covering AI policy awareness, observed student AI use, confidence in understanding AI risks, and current guidance received from schools.

🎙️

Qualitative Interviews

8 in-depth interviews selected to reflect variation in school type, geographic context, and level of prior AI engagement. Interviews surfaced attitudes, barriers, and what parents actually want from schools on this topic.

Secondary Data Triangulation

  • Center for Democracy & Technology (CDT) — student privacy and AI governance research
  • Common Sense Media — youth digital wellbeing and parental awareness data
  • Walton-GSV-Gallup — national K-12 AI adoption and stakeholder readiness tracking

Outputs: What Districts and Edtech Need to Do

The research produces an overview of risks and opportunities for district leaders and edtech executives — focused on closing the parent communication gap through concrete policy and product changes.

📣
Parent Communication Design

79% of surveyed parents believe responsibility for children's AI education should be shared between schools and parents, they just need to be included

⚠️
Product Gaps

A prioritized list of what parents want to learn and their confidence gap based on K-12 parent responses

🔧
Implementation Pathways

Sequenced steps for districts at different stages of AI adoption, from no policy to mature governance
